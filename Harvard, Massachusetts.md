# Harvard, Massachusetts

## The Weirdest Town in the World

What you are about to read is 100% true. Many factions with vast underground networks of influence have worked to keep this knowledge hidden for decades and in some cases centuries. Though it brings upon me great peril to write this book, I feel it is my duty to relay the truth to you, the reader. This is the real story of a small New England town which is at the nexus of magic, mystery, government black site operations, and the occult.

The town of Harvard, Massachusetts (named after, but otherwise no relation to the university, which is in Cambridge) is the weirdest, most mysterious, and spookiest little town in Massachusetts. You may be thinking “Wait, what about Salem?” Sure, maybe 250 years ago, Salem was spooky, what with the vestiges of the Salem witch trials. But that was then, and just one point in time. Today, Salem is a giant tourist trap that real witches wouldn’t even fly over, let alone visit.

Harvard is a quaint little New England town of about 5,000 people in central Massachusetts. Its history goes back to 1658 when it was first settled as farmland by the Dillard family, who are a pillar of the community to this day. It has all the character – and mystery – of a traditional small New England town. 

Harvard’s characteristic rolling hills make it a popular location for cyclists to explore. This despite the many unexplained disappearances, which is why most cyclists now ride in large packs through town. Locals know the roads to avoid, and if asked, might warn off an errant cyclist from biking down avenues known to be dangerous, like Cleaves Hill and Lover’s Lane.

The Harvard General Store is a popular hangout for the locals and those coming to visit the area’s many apple orchards. Just make sure you never come to town alone, and don’t stay past dusk. 

## Devens

When European colonists first came to the area in the early 1600s, the local Narragansett tribe called the valley just past the Nashua River "Chapiokhen", and seemed to never hunt or travel in the area. In 1668, when the first residents built houses in what is now Harvard, they called the area beyond the Nashua River “Shabikin”. Only much later would some indigenous language scholars realize that Chapiokhen means "hades" or "place of the dead".

A smattering of houses would be built in the Shabikin area of Harvard, but strange occurrences happened there more than even the rest of Harvard \- stories of disappearances of people and livestock, strange sounds and sights in the woods, and crops that were twisted and deformed. 

In 1917, this northwest portion of Harvard was partitioned off by the federal government to establish a United States Army military installation called Fort Devens. The officially reported purpose of the base was for training draftees for World War I. Despite only being for “training”, hundreds of deaths would be reported at the base in its very first year, all listed as “recruits”.

20 years later, residents of Harvard would relate stories about roaring and bright lights coming from the base, which the government only explained as “scientific experimentation”.

During World War II, the government spent millions constructing many large buildings on the site for purposes unknown. The number of people and trucks going in and out increased dramatically during this time, and security was tightened even further than it had been.

According to what little information is available from the government, in the 1950s through the 1970s, the fort was used as training grounds for various Army Security and Army Intelligence agencies.

Due to extensive environmental contamination which was never properly explained by the Army, Fort Devens was listed as a superfund site in 1989 and closed a few years thereafter…. Or so the official reports say.

Many local residents believe that the experiments performed at the base have never stopped. On a regular basis, Harvard residents still hear loud booms from the fort that shake the very foundations of their houses. Unmarked military trucks continue to go in and out of the closed base. Guards on site say they are keeping people out of the area for their own safety due to unexplained “hazards” still remaining on the base.

### Mirror Lake aka Hell Pond

In a corner of the area originally known as Shabikin was a small but incredibly deep body of water the local native american tribe called Hell Pond. No one knows why it was called this, but they know it is incredibly deep for a relatively small pond. Perhaps a glacial anomaly, the lake is about 150’ across and at least 80’ deep in parts. Some people say that in the deepest parts, it connects up to an underground cave network.

When the military took over the area in 1917, the name of the pond was changed to Mirror Lake, but many locals still call it by its original name 

### Commonwealth Nuclear Systems

​​Recently, Commonwealth Nuclear Systems, a startup run by a group of ex-MIT scientists and funded by dark money venture capitalists began building a 50 acre nuclear fusion facility in an unused portion of the old Devens base. Their stated goal is combining 1950s soviet nuclear fusion technology with modern ARC reactors, but on a much larger scale \- nicknamed SPARC (Super Powered ARC). The Russian government has objected strenuously to this use of their scientific data, and reports say the Department of Energy has had to step in and add a security detail to the facility.

## The New Library (Old Bromfield)

The new library used to be the old Bromfield school, back when one small building could hold all the children in the town. It’s a beautiful brick affair, sitting behind a green field, next to the high school, and overlooking the eastern end of Bare Hill Pond. It was expanded and converted into a library after the town built the current highschool to manage the growing population of students. However, some say that you can still hear the screams of the school janitor that was accidentally locked in the basement one summer. 

### Library Director

Mary Williams \- shoulder length straight hair, glasses. In her 70s but still spry and sharp as a tack. She’s an expert on all things occult (and many things mundane). Mary has a secret stash of occult books and a small collection of occult paraphernalia of local and historic value that she might be willing to share access to, if you prove yourself worthy.
